Lecture #11: Revelations & Christian Apocalyptic

  • Christianity commenced its career as an eschatological and enthusiastic movement and gave expression to its faith largely in the language of Apocalyptic and of prophecy.
  • From the end of the 1st century to the beginning of the Middle Ages it produced an extensive apocalyptic literature.
  • Prophecy was not as productive, but it was also widely produced and had historical significance.
  • Christianity took over the genre of the Apocalypse from Palestinian Judaism and that of the Sibyllines from Hellenistic Judaism.
  • The only apocalypse (besides the small ones in the synoptics) found in the New Testament is Revelation of St. John.
  • However, next to gospels and acts of apostles, apocalypses are the most frequently written books of the New Testament apocrypha.

  • All apocalypses have some things in common, including Rev.
  • Pseudonymity-the writer does not use his own name but takes the name of a famous personage of the past. along with this a fictitious antiquity is found as an element in the style. This book only recently becomes known because of the command for its secret being sealed till the end of days.
  • Account of the vision. Visions predominate so strongly that the apocalypses are generally presented in the form of an account of a vision, this can be in a dream or ecstasy or a waking state.
  • Surveys of history in future form. related to the fiction of antiquity is the fact that the apocalyptic writers frequently present the history of the past right up to their own time in the form of prophecies. This is always followed by a prediction of the end, and on this the emphasis lies: the present of the actual (not fictional) author is always the last time.
  • All apocalypses include exhortations to repentance and conversion in view of the imminent end and of judgment, as well as traditional ethical exhortations in the form of maxims and series of aphorisms which are sometimes arranged thematically.
  • Apocalypses contain not only revelations on the last things, (including the termination of history) but also on other secrets, on the Beyond, Heaven and Hell and their inhabitants, on astronomy, meteorology, and geography, as well as on the origin of sin and evil in the world.

  • The main interest does not lie in problems of cosmology or evil, but in eschatology -- the end of things.
  • The world of ideas in apocalyptic is very varied but some common features are:
  • The doctrine of the two ages. There is always a contrast between this age and the age to come.

This age is perishable, the age to come eternal and comes by a break in from beyond in supernatural fashion, thru divine intervention and w/o human activity, and puts an end to this world-era.

The end is extreme: it is judgment, a destruction of this world and the simultaneous appearance of a new one, "new heaven and a new earth.

  • Pessimism and hope of the beyond. The other worldly character of the coming Age implies an extreme devaluation of this age, the so-called apocalyptic pessimism.

This is symbolized by the idea of the dominion over it by Satan and evil powers and growing physical and moral degeneration.

The natural aging of the world and the moral and religious decay of humankind culminate in a cosmic catastrophe in which this age perishes and the New comes. it reaches from the

  • Universalism and Individualism. creation to the dissolution of the world and include, all not just earth. each individual must be judged by God not as a Jew or gentile but an individual.
  • Determinism and imminent expectation. alongside dualism, this is the outstanding characteristic. God has fore-ordained everything: all that happens precisely according to the fixed plan of God, which human plans and actions can neither advance nor hinder.

God is the God of both ages. in fact, he created all things at the same time, even the end, the coming age which now sleeps. all is pre-existent and the seer can see them either in the world above or descending from it.

Since everything has its time precisely determined, the end of this age can be calculated, either by reckoning its entire duration from the creation or by reckoning from a point within history or by observing the signs of the times.

But these calculations are always determined by the conviction that the end is very near at hand.

  • Finally, there is a lack of uniformity in the expressions each author takes.

Variations in the concept of a savior figure is a case.

In national eschatology, a messiah who is in the line of David is present as the savior but in many apocalypses God or an angel of God brings in the salvation and judgment and replaces the old age with the new.

At times the transcendent judge and redeemer figure, the son of man draws to himself many of the titles and traits of the Davidic Messiah (Enoch and Esdras.)

However, the Davidic messiah is not allowed to disappear completely.

There are many other themes which vary, but the theme of the Savior has been briefly summarized here because of the significance in Christian apocalyptic.

The Book of Revelation

  • Revelation is the last book in the N. T. and the only one that is completely apocalyptic in nature.
  • John's Apocalypse far transcends all others of its class in the endeavor to present a theodicy or theology of history.

to portray both "what is and what is to take place (1:19) in the perspective of God's eternal purpose relative to man and his salvation.

  • Basically, the book of Revelation is in the form of a letter.
  • What it appears is that John wrote the book as a drama, similar to the Greek dramas of his day with 7 acts each containing 7 scenes and that thereafter, with a view to bringing it to the church's attention, he superimposed the letter form upon it.
  • John claims for his work a close affinity in spirit and content with the writings of the Hebrew prophets as well
  • Thus, he mentions the book being apocalyptic once, and of being prophecy six times placing himself in the category of the "prophets." (22:7)

  • John's teaching purports to be far closer to that of the Hebrew prophetic scriptures than is that of Enoch, II Baruch, and the Sibylline Oracles.
  • He quotes directly phrases and clauses from the Hebrew prophets in some 150 passages.

  • This saturation with the Hebrew prophetic thought-frames and symbolism may well be why his book was included in the New Testament canon when others of this type were not.
  • As far as style and diction are concerned, there are many errors and it appears that the writer thought in grammatical Aramaic and wrote in Greek.

It is by far the poorest Greek in the New Testament

Who wrote it?

  • The author calls himself John, but it is not John the Disciple of Jesus.
  • This John was the pastor of at least the "seven churches" of Asia and he wrote to them out of a shepherd's concern for his flock while exiled from them on the Isle of Patmos

  • What we know about this John is that he was very likely a Jewish Believer in Jesus as the Messiah. He would not have known the word Christian, or if he did, he didn't accept it.
  • In his letters to the seven churches, he approves only of the church at Philadelphia. He sees the others as upstarts claiming they are the equal of Israel. He never mentions Paul, but in his letters, he strongly disapproves of those who eat meat sacrificed to idols, and other practices that Paul said did not matter.
  • He may well have seen James, the brother of Jesus, killed in Jerusalem and certainly knew the temple and its worship before it was destroyed in 70 AD. John's first language was Aramaic or Hebrew and he wrote in Greek. He was very worried about contamination by Roman culture. He calls the Churches that have compromised "synagogues of Satan" because they eat meat sacrificed to idols and practice fornication.

Methods of interpretation

  • Needless to say a variety of methods have been used to interpret Revelation
  • Not only does the book seem an enigma in itself in view of it visionary pattern, its curious figures (beasts, dragon, horsemen, hordes of demons, angels, and the rest), and its cosmic catastrophes; but it has much to say as well of the eschatological time (the end time), a subject about which the church has never made up it collective mind satisfactorily.
  • Thus, Christian scholars have always approached a study of Revelation with minds biased on the subjects with which it deals and the net result has been chaotic.

Historico-eschatological interpretations

  • There are several ways of doing this
  • First, a contemporary chronological scheme so that the whole sweep of his visionary program is limited to his own times, the time of Domitian.
  • Futuristic – i.e., limited entirely to the eschatological time at the end of history, a time identified on this view with the period immediately preceding, contemporary with, and following upon the "thousand years" literally interpreted (20:1-10)
  • Hal Lindsey, the Jehovah Witnesses among others, tend to interpret it this way.

  • Then the generally historical, either in the sense that the seer as a philosopher of history endeavors to present the whole panorama of history from the divine standpoint, as was done by other apocalyptists, or that he predicts specific events as about to happen throughout the span of world history, or that he has in mind certain characteristic types of the divine redemptive activity which find repeated expression throughout history and likewise characteristic human attempts to resist and thwart the divine will.

Literary-analytical approaches

  • Some have seen the book as the end product of a series of editors who have worked to assemble many sources
  • Allegorical Method
  • Is from the Alexandrian school of biblical interpretation.
  • This works better with Revelation than about any other book because of its nature, however,
  • This method fails to serve any useful purpose when the bounds set by John's ideological scheme are transgressed.
  • Dramatic Interpretation
  • That John's book is highly dramatic in style has long been recognized.
  • The book readily divides itself into seven Acts, each Act having in turn seven Scenes and so to divide it is to follow out the original intent of the author.
  • Act I gives the picture of the church contemporary with the author.
  • Acts IT-VII similarly present us with "what is to take place hereafter these several methods are not mutually exclusive.

  • The problem in interpreting this book has resulted from the effort to discover a chronological sequence in this series of visions.
  • This has been true in the futuristic way which tries to see in these visions’ pictures of the successive situations that will arise in the last days and the historica which relates the visions to the ongoing history of the church and identifies the symbolic figures of the book with historical personages.

  • The futuristic method ignores the clear historical allusions of the writing. and the historical method must constantly be revised with the passage of time since as historical crises pass it becomes clear that there was no basis for seeing in them the fulfillment of John's prophecies.
