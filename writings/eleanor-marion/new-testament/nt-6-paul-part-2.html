Wisdom

  • Paul's detractors in Corinth were threatening to distort the gospel by a triumphalism that implied an inner group had the knowledge to overcome the unseen spiritual beings and were thus better than the average christian.
  • These people apparently claimed that the secret knowledge it possessed provided the sole means of man's salvation and that Paul's message lacked the requisite knowledge. thus Paul, after denouncing the cult of personality that divided the church there, begins an argument calculated to interest the greeks (Read: I Cor 1:22)
  • He shows that God chose to reveal the truth thru a message that is devoid of wisdom as measured by human standards (I Cor 1:26)
  • Unlike a philosophers persuasive rational arguments, Paul's message centers on the symbol of human weakness: the Cross
  • The fact that Jesus had died suspended to a wooden cross "hanged on a tree" (Gal 3:13) placed him under a curse according to Jewish law (Deut 21:23)
  • for Jews, then, the notion of a crucified Messiah was a stumbling block (I Cor 1:23) to their acceptance.
  • Paul insists that God uses the message of the Cross for man's redemption because of its stress on weakness.

  • He chose one who was obedient unto death (Phil 2:8) allowed him to be executed as a criminal (Gal 3:13) and chose Paul, a weak person, to be the chief messenger to the gentiles.
  • WHY? not because God had no other means, but because he did not want the redemption of man to rest in any way on human strengths or virtues.
  • only this way would arrogant humans be brought to acknowledge their dependence on God.

Wisdom for the Mature

  • Paul asserts that there is for Christians a hidden wisdom, but it is not available either for outsiders or for the immature among those within the community. God.
  • It is accessible to man only as a gift of the Spirit of God

  • The natural mind (psychikos) or the flesh (sarkikos) cannot come to this knowledge except by the Spirit of God.
  • Paul did not share the gnostic view that the material world was inherently evil, but instead believed that when man was redeemed the whole creation would be redeemed as well (Rom 8:19-23)

  • the wisdom which Paul imparts, or which he believes God imparts thru His Spirit is not merely concerned with the It has, rather, direct bearing on such otherworldly. practical affairs of this world as personal morality, marriage, legal disputes, earning a living, eating dinner with unconverted friends, complaining about one's lot, getting drunk at community gatherings.
  • It is on these specific matters that Paul utters the wisdom granted him for the benefit of the churches under his charge.

Vision of God

  • The initial vision of God thru which Paul received knowledge of the gospel was in the revelation of the risen Christ (Gal 1:12, 16)
  • But the mystical vision of God is continued, Paul asserts (Read II Cor 3:4-18) through the work of the Spirit.
  • he uses Moses veiling his face, not to hide the glory of God but because he was embarrassed that it was fading.
  • This is what has happened to the old covenant, says Paul. but, the new covenant, the glory increases and the faces of those transformed by that glory are not veiled.
  • The divine radiance is now to be seen in the change that is taking place in the faithful as they take on more and more of the divine likeness in which they were created.
  • This is not an everlasting process: its goal is "becoming like him" in his death and resurrection (Phil 3:10) an objective which will only be fully achieved at the end of the age.
  • This transforming knowledge that Paul is striving for is not knowing about Jesus, but knowing him (Phil 3:8) which for Paul has surpassing worth. What he seeks is direct mystical experience, not merely intellectual information about Jesus, and through him of God.

  • In offering wisdom to the mature, Paul is himself on the way to maturity for as he says he has not yet attained his full potential.

The Eschaton

  • For Paul wisdom is not so much a matter of information as of mystical insight and illumination, there is nevertheless, certain important items available only to people of faith.
  • Significant among these is the disclosure of the details of the circumstances that will lead to the consummation of God's purpose at the end of the age.
  • The basis for Paul's eschatological experience is that shared with others, ie, Jesus' appearance raised from the dead by God.
  • So fundamental was this belief in the Resurrection that Paul could not stand by quietly and allow its validity to be questioned at Corinth (see 15:)
  • He argues that as a Pharisee, he already believed in the resurrection of the dead before becoming a Christian and that the Corinthian peoples' experience was valid of the saving grace of God. But then he says, to his own direct experience, he knows God raised Jesus because he saw him.
  • Because of this great event he knows that all will be resurrected as Christ was the guarantee.
  • Hellenistic Christianity would have a hard time accepting a resurrection since the Greeks believed only the soul survived death, thus the difficulty in dealing with the concept of bodily resurrection.
  • At most, the soul would transmigrate from one body to the next in reincarnation (plato) but the body per se did not matter.
  • Paul does not mean the body will be identical with the one a person has when he dies, but it will be a tangible, physical substance, and identifiable as a personal self which can be distinguished from others.
  • The body is exchanged (I Cor 15:35-39) and bodies differ not merely in a general way, but in glory, as exemplified by the way that the glory of the sun differs from the moon or stars. (15:41)
  • In I Thess 4, Paul speaks of the dead in Christ being raised to meet the Lord in the air along with those still alive. This, of course, is what is referred to as the rapture.

  • The meeting in the air is probably an allusion to the figure in Dan 7, where "one like the Son of Man" appears on the clouds of heaven on his way to earth to receive authority over God's kingdom.
  • The saints of I Thess 4 are therefore the hosts who accompany the eschatological King to earth to receive the kingdom.
  • The resurrection hope thus served both as a reminder of the shortcomings that Paul could observe in his own present way of life and as a future goal of what he might yet become.
  • with this prospect before him, he was striving for ever increasing maturity in the faith.

  • Before continuing, I want to point out that in antiquity pseudonyms. authorship was a widely accepted literary convention
  • Therefore, the use of an apostle's name in reasserting his teaching (or what was believed to be his teaching,) was not regarded as dishonest but merely a way of reminding the church of what it had received from God through that apostle.
  • Also – the authority of the N. T. books are not dependent on their human authorship but what the church deemed as the authentic voice of apostolic teaching.

Pauline letters

The Pastorals

  • I and II Timothy and Titus have been called pastorals because they contain much material related to the pastoral oversight of the churches.
  • They are closely similar to each other in content, diction, and theology.
  • They lack the typical theological motifs of Paul such as justification by faith, redemption through the death of Christ on the cross, and use of other terms in ways foreign to paul.
  • Faith, as in James, does not define a relationship to Christ but signifies a body of doctrine which must be kept free of perversion
  • the main argument for a late first or early 2nd century writing of these is:
  • the Episcopes or Bishop is now the church leader and there is a regularly paid local minister (I tim 5:17) which was not true under Paul
  • women were no longer allowed the freedom they had under the earlier church, but were commanded to be submissive, as they would be "saved by childbearing" (I Tim 2:15)
  • This was not Paul's view, who believed women and men could equally be called on by the spirit to speak, prophecy, etc. (see I Cor 11)
  • As a matter of fact, the passage in I Cor 14:33b-36 is believed by scholars to be an interpolation from this period into the genuine Pauline letter to give Paul authority to women being silenced.

  • Thus, the movement toward hierarchy, the opposition to Gnosticism (which I will discuss later) that was far more developed in this period, and the change in the attitude towards women all point to a late writing of these three letters.

II Thessalonians

  • There is not much question about whether Paul wrote I Thess, but his authorship of II Thess has been disputed on several grounds.
  • First, in I Thess Paul, while emphasizing that the Parousia (see I thess 2:19) will come unexpectedly, speaks as if it were near
  • II Thess on the other hand, indicates that there will be considerable delay between his letter and the Parousia, and he gives detailed signs which must occur first.
  • Second, the strongest argument for inauthenticity is that this letter speaks against the teaching that "the day of the Lord is already here" (2:2).
  • It is difficult to suppose that this latter view, which treats the Parousia as a present spiritual experience, would flourish in Thessalonica at the same time as the opposite, the fervent expectation of the Lord's coming as a future event, found in I Thess.
  • Nevertheless, it is possible that Paul responded to a new problem which arose after the writing of the earlier letter.

Colossians

  • There were several of the later epistles dealing with Gnosticism, but they were all much later and were dealing with Gnosticism from the viewpoint of the institutional church of the late first and early 2nd centuries.
  • The only one that might have been Paul was Col, and it was much earlier, whether Paul or not, than any of the others.

  • the Christians of Colossae in Asia minor were already showing Gnostic tendencies, a proto-Gnosticism, if you will.
  • They believed that these elemental spirits all contained part of the Pleroma or fullness of the Godhead and that they were the Cause of creation and shared control of the earth and human destiny.
  • Thus, one had to find out who controlled him and how to conform to them to fulfill one's destiny.
  • Paul tries to counter these tendencies by saying Christ, not these elemental spirits, contains all the fullness of God and that Christ by his death had won a victory over these forces
  • Paul never denies their existence, as most people did, but is convinced that God is fully in control through Christ.
  • He appeals to the wisdom literature, proverbs 8, only he says Christ was the firstborn of all creation, thus equating wisdom with Christ.
